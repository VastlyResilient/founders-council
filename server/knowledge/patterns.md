## HOW TO USE THIS DOCUMENT (read this first)

You run live businesses. This document exists for one reason: **so that in six months you can look at a market and recognize a shape without me.** Everything below is built to transfer the skill, not to display work.

Rules of engagement, from the operating brief, restated because they are the whole method:

1. **Survivorship bias is assumed until proven otherwise.** This corpus contains only winners. Frugality, persistence, obsession appear in bankrupt founders at similar rates. A claim is a *candidate* until counterexamples are found.
2. **Never accept a conclusion without its evidence trail.** Every pattern card cites founders, episodes, industries, eras, and named counterexamples. You can check me and disagree with me. A conclusion you can't audit is one you can't learn from.
3. **Do not confirm what you want to be true.** The point is to break it.
4. **Tag the source.** Decisions documented at the time ≠ biographies written in hindsight. Memoirs are self-serving. Retrofitted stories are tagged HINDSIGHT in this document.
5. **Say "not stated."** Gaps are not filled with inference dressed as fact.
6. **Contradictions are findings.** When two founders did opposite things and both won, the conditions that selected each are the lesson.
7. **Delete aggressively.** The graveyard (Section 2) is as important as the library.

The three registries (Section 8) — predictions, anomalies, recall — are the scorekeeping without which none of this works.

# SECTION 2 — THE GRAVEYARD (patterns killed this cycle)

These were tested against the corpus and failed. Deleting them is progress. **Do not rediscover these dead ends.**

| Pattern (killed) | Why it died |
|---|---|
| **"Obsession/persistence wins"** — the "relentless founder" story | WEAK SIGNAL. Present in virtually every survivor and in most bankrupt founders too. Standard Instrument #407 (Charles Kettering), Dyson, Jobs were relentlessly persistent — but so was nearly every Chapter 7 filer. The trait does not discriminate. Killed as a *causal* pattern; retained as a *hygiene* factor only. |
| **"Frugality produces success"** | Cannot survive counterexample search. Frugality is a *symptom* of capital scarcity, not a success engine. Rockefeller's early frugality is real but so is the splurge-then-survive profile of many founders. Not distinguishable from sampling noise. Rejected. |
| **"Founders take huge personal risk"** | Confounded: constraint, not choice. What looks like bold risk is usually *the only option available to a person with no downside left*. Killed. |
| **"The vision always precedes the market"** | Belied by corpus: most decisive moves were reactions to a measured, present customer need (Bezos stalking booksellers; Buffett buying what's cheap). Vision-first is HINDSIGHT framing. Killed as a general rule. |
| **"Down rounds / giving up equity is failure"** | Both can be moves. Ellison and Jobs gave up huge equity portions and won; others refused and died. The *refusal itself* is not the pattern; the condition under which you can refuse is. Re-FRAMED, not killed — see contradiction map. |
| **"Move fast / be first-to-market wins"** | Killed. First-mover data is strongly mixed; several late entrants in corpus (Amazon into books was *not* first; Walmart into discount — not first) beat first movers by out-executing on the *second* order problem. The real shape is earlier → different axis. |

**Meta-lesson from the graveyard:** every pattern that died is a statement *about the person* (they were persistent, brave, frugal, visionary). Statements about the person are infinitely retrofittable. The patterns that survive are statements about *the structure of the situation*. If a rule reads like a compliment, it's dead.

# SECTION 3 — THE CONTRADICTION MAP (validated patterns pointing opposite ways)

**Conditions select each side.** Do not resolve these into false consensus.

## C1: Concentrate vs. Diversify
- **Side A (concentrate):** Rockefeller: "Singleness of purpose." Sam Walton: hedgehog, one store learned to the floorboards. Sao Paulo (one trick). Dyson: 15 years one product. 
- **Side B (diversify):** The Great Family Dynasties (#307): spread across *unrelated* businesses (Rockefeller, Rothschild, Morgan, Toyoda diversified within holding structures). Kroc: McDonald's franchise spread as the *business model itself*.
- **Selection condition:** Concentration wins when the *bottleneck* is one specific capability you uniquely hold (distribution, craft, a customer segment) and that capability faces a clear runway. Diversification wins when (a) the single business is *capital-bound and saturating* (holding-company logic), or (b) risk is concentrated in one *undiversifiable* factor (regulatory, single-customer).
- **Backfire:** Concentrate into a disappearing runway = Kodak. Diversify before bottleneck is mastered = Sheraton-style sprawl with no engine.

## C2: Raise cheap capital broadly vs. starve yourself / stay lean
- **Side A (raise):** Bezos, Musk — heavy capital, willing dilution, because they bought *time* and *distribution*.
- **Side B (stay lean):** Sam Walton, In-N-Out, DHH/37signals — no outside capital, growth slower but owned.
- **Selection condition:** Raise when the *competitive clock* is set by capital-intensive scale (network effects, infrastructure, distribution density). Stay lean when the moat is *craft/quality* and the market is fine-grained (many small customers), such that capital buys little.
- **Backfire:** Raising in a craft market buys nothing but dilution (DHH's explicit point). Staying lean in a winner-take-all network market concedes the game (also DHH — he chose NOT to play that game, which is the point: he picked the market where lean wins).

## C3: Sell to the mass / cheap (Sam Walton, Ford) vs. sell premium to the few (Red Bull's Mateschitz, Estée Lauder)
- **Selection condition:** Mass-cheap wins when the category is *experienced as a commodity* and the bottleneck is distribution/volume (ignore niche: who cares). Premium wins when the product is *identity-bearing* — the buyer is buying status/self-image, where price *is* the signal (Lauder, Red Bull at 3x cost, Jobs).
- **Backfire:** Premium price on a commodity = no volume, no margin. Mass price on an identity product = kills the signal, kills demand. This is nearly always a *category call*, not a strategy call.

# SECTION 4 — THE CONDITIONS BOARD (his monthly working tool)

**Run this against any market, monthly. A shape fires when three or more of its conditions co-occur AND nothing is priced for it.**

This board is a scaffold — Section 1 (the library) fills each row with the specific conditions per pattern. The board is the *checklist form* of the library.

### The firing-condition checklist (populate from Section 1 as patterns validate)

1. Is there a **bottleneck** — one link in a chain everyone needs but nobody owns / nobody finds interesting? (Rockefeller: pipelines & railcars)
2. Is there something **mispriced or perceived as boring** that the consensus is ignoring? (Walton: small-town retail; Buffett: "boring" insurers)
3. Is distribution/density the actual constraint, and are competitors fighting on a *different* axis (product shine, not reach)?
4. Is the market **consolidating or scattered** — and is the mess itself the opportunity? (Kroc: chaotic independent hamburger stands)
5. Is there a **macro force** (regulation change, technology, capital cycle, demographics) with a *defined direction* underriding the market?
6. Is a competitor treasury-rich but **structurally unable** to defend a niche you can own (their cost base forbids the niche's price point)?
7. Is the "consensus view" backed by *identical evidence* to the contrarian view — i.e., is the disagreement purely about **weighting**, not facts?
8. Does something that "shouldn't work" keep working across ≥3 unrelated cases? → Anomaly log (Section 8).

**The veto checklist (backfire conditions):**
- Would the move's success depend on a **one-time regulatory window** (ERA-LOCKED) just because it looks permanent?
- Is the "boring" thing actually **structurally unprofitable** (not merely mispriced)?
- Does concentration point at a **disappearing** runway?
- Am I being asked to believe a **personal** trait (their persistence) caused the outcome, with no structural mechanism?

# SECTION 5 — THE TEMPERAMENT REPORT (which of these can YOU run?)

A pattern you are temperamentally unable to execute is worth nothing to you. This section states — directly — which patterns require which temperament, and which become *unavailable* to you without each one. **Do not skip this section by telling yourself you have all of them.**

## T1. The ability to *endure ugly* (Collapse-behavior pattern)
- **Required for:** any pattern where you hold position through a drawdown against consensus — the contrarian call (C2's Side A), long-horizon customer work, waiting out a market. 
- **Unavailable without it:** you cannot hold the "buy it cheap and wait" shape if a 20% drawdown makes you sell. You will convert a structural pattern into a trade and lose the edge.
- **Where it's STRONG:** the specific move it enables is *refusing to liquidate a thesis because it's uncomfortable*. That's a decision, not a personality — it can be trained by making the liquidation rule explicit in advance (see Prediction log, Section 8).

## T2. Relentless *specific* attention to the boring link
- **Required for:** the bottleneck patterns (Section 1, the "boring link" family).
- **Unavailable without it:** you can identify the bottleneck but you will not *stay* on it; you'll drift to the interesting problem and leave the load-bearing boring work half-done. The pattern fails in your hands even though you ran the market correctly.
- **Self-test before adopting:** can you point to a boring thing you have sustained for 3 months without an audience reward? If no, this family is not executable by you *yet*.

## T3. The refusal to be the explainer / the seller-when-it's-not-fun
- **Required for:** bootstrap-and-owned patterns (DHH, In-N-Out) and for the "sell to the mass" patterns (C3 Side A).
- **Unavailable without it:** with a different temperament (wanting the interesting customer, hating rejection) the mass-cheap shape becomes agony and you under-execute volume.

## T4. Equanimity under *personal* cost (family/health/backstop)
- Report what the record shows: many founders (and virtually all "enduring" ones) ran on the labor of someone else (a spouse, a partner, a backstop) — load-bearing and noted in the corpus. **Estee Lauder, Jobs, nearly all have a backstop line.**
- Moralizing removed, the useful question: *who absorbs the other 50% of your life while you do this, and is that person a real, durable backstop or a band-aid?* A pattern that costs you your backstop kills itself. Run the personal-cost block through the same graduation bar — and note the founders who *avoided* the cost: Walton (drove home daily; married early, stable), Buffett (same wife 50 years, worked from Omaha). Avoiding the cost appears *more* the pattern than enduring it.

## T5. Scorekeeping (the one non-negotiable)
- Every pattern in this document depends on the registries in Section 8. The temperament required is *not* intelligence — it is the willingness to record a dated, falsifiable call (and often be wrong publicly to yourself). **If you will not keep the log, stop here.** Nothing in this document survives unlogged.

# SECTION 6 — THE COURSE (taught prediction-first, oldest eras first)

**How to run this course:** one session per sitting, max three patterns per session. NEVER read the reveal (Section 7) before committing to your call. Each session opens with the situation as the founder faced it — you commit, *then* you read what happened. The gap between your call and theirs is the lesson. Being right for the wrong reason is a failure — the answer key grades your *reasoning*, not your conclusion.

**Curriculum order (deliberate):**
1. **Oldest eras first** — Rockefeller, Vanderbilt, Ford. Their tactics are unusable in your world, which forces you to extract the *shape* instead of copying the move.
2. **Failure and stagnation early** — Insull, Kodak-era Polaroid. Clean signal; builds your veto list before your taste for wins.
3. **Modern founders last** (Bezos, Musk, DHH). Only once you have shapes to slot their moves into.

## SESSION 1 — THE BOTTLENECK, AND THE MAN WHO MADE THE RULES
*(Pattern to acquire; taught via Rockefeller, 1870s — old era, deep in the past)*

**THE SHAPE:** *Took control of the boring link in the chain that everyone on both sides of it depended on, then set terms for everyone — including people upstream and downstream who thought they were his customers or suppliers.*
- fires when: a scattered set of producers and buyers all depend on one unglamorous link (transport, credit, distribution, standard-setting)
- backfires when: the link is controlled by a monopoly that can't be bought (either side), OR the link's economics are genuinely unprofitable, OR regulation later caps the link (railroads: the 1906 rate crackdown is the backfire that took Standard Oil apart — the shape wore permanent clothes, the era's form didn't)
- proves: Rockefeller (pipelines & railcars, oil), and — modern echoes — Buffett's insurers (float), Google/Amazon (distribution and identity plumbing)
- disproves: [counterexample search noted in answer key — the corpus contains no Standard Oil-style failures; that's why it's CANDIDATE until outside search confirms, per brief]

**THE SITUATION (1868-ish; commit before reading on):**
> You are a young man in the first decade of a chaotic new industry — oil. Through trial you learn: the territory is full of fast-growing, disorganized producers and refiners, and the one thing everyone needs — moving the product from the fields to the refiners and to the market — is fragmented, unreliable, and run by people who treat you as one more customer. The market is scattered, prices swing wildly, and a handful of well-known players are competing on who can refine a barrel slightly cheaper. The "intelligent consensus" says: win by making a better barrel, cheaper, or producing the best crude source.
> **What do you do? Commit to your answer before continuing.** (Mark it with a date in your prediction log.)

*(Answer and reasoning comparison: Section 7, Session 1.)*

**WHO PROVES IT:** Rockefeller (#148, #16, #368) — took the railcars/pipeline link; Buffett-style compounding on boring necessity. Ford (later) — took the supply chain link in autos.
**WHO DISPROVES IT:** *(outside-corpus candidates required; corpus has none by construction — see answer key note; mark CANDIDATE.)*
**YOUR TURN:** *Name the boring link **your** local market (or the one you're actually in) runs on, that nobody owns, and what would have to be true for you to buy it.*



## SESSION 2 — THE SCATTERED MARKET AND THE STANDARDIZER
*(Taught via Ray Kroc / McDonald's, 1950s.)*

**THE SHAPE:** *Standardized a wildly-variable, decentralized category from the top down — not by owning the outlets but by owning the rules, the supply, and the real estate — while everyone else bickered over the individual store.*

**THE SITUATION (1954-ish):**
> You're a 52-year-old milkshake-machine salesman. Your best customers are a pair of brothers running one wildly successful hamburger stand in California. The food world around you is completely scattered: thousands of independent burger joints, each doing things its own way, wildly inconsistent, no national standard, most barely surviving. Industry consensus: "food is local; you can't scale a good hamburger; franchises are unreliable." You notice something the brothers do that no one else does: they run the place like a system — a limited menu, a repeatable process, a spotless operation.
> **What do you do? Commit before continuing.**

*(Reveal + reasoning comparison: Section 7, Session 2.)*

**WHO PROVES IT:** Kroc (#7, #293) — owned the franchise + real estate + standards; Walton (later, same shape in retail, scattered small towns); In-N-Out (#244) — the other branch of the same shape (owned, tiny menu, standards).
**WHO DISPROVES IT:** most franchise food systems of the 1960s (the failed standardizers) and — outside corpus — Pizza chain implosions; the shape backfires when you standardize *before* the process is actually repeatable or when you franchise without real estate ownership (you just license a name, you don't own the bottleneck) → you get the licensing-fee model that dies with the franchisee.
**FIRES WHEN:** category is scattered AND the winning unit economics are already proven in one location (repeatable, even if small) AND capital exists to own the reinforcing link (real estate, supply, brand).
**BACKFIRES WHEN:** you own rules but not the load-bearing asset (a name alone is not a moat) OR you standardize a category whose value is *variety* (myth: everyone wants the same burger — the shape needs the category to actually reward consistency).
**YOUR TURN:** *name a scattered category you touch weekly. Is the inconsistency itself the complaint, or is that a story the standardizer tells? What would "owning the rules" buy you in it?*

## SESSION 3 — THE FAILURE EPISODE, FIRST (the veto-list builder)
*(Taught early on purpose. From your corpus's own failure episodes: Insull — "How To Lose A Few Billion Dollars.")*

**THE SHAPE (negative):** *A man who once owned the boring link (electric power, and its financing) expanded from owning the link into owning everything downstream of it on borrowed money, betting the debt load on perpetual cheap capital and perpetual growth — the exact bet mature infra cannot survive.*

**THE SITUATION (teach FIRST as what to veto):**
> You built a utility empire at the dawn of mass electricity. For decades you were the honest, boring, utility monopolist — the safe compounder — and you were genuinely excellent at it. Then the game changed: a stock-market boom makes utility equity and debt cheap and abundant. Everyone tells you the era of "just own the power plant" is over and the future is the **horizontal holding company** — own power generation, plus every downstream consumer, stacked in pyramids of holding companies, financed with debt that rolls cheaply.
> **What do you do? Commit before continuing.**

*(Reveal: Section 7, Session 3.)*

**WHY THIS SESSION FIRST:** this veto — *don't lever the boring link into endless downstream on cheap debt* — is worth more than every "how to win" session. Failure defines the boundary of the permanent shape (Session 1) precisely. Session 1's shape backfires *exactly here*: the link stops being the link the moment you stop owning just the link.
**YOUR TURN:** *where is your own load-bearing asset, and what cheap-debt "expand downstream" story is being sold to you or is tempting you to finance current operations with future growth?*

## SESSION 4 — THE MIS-PRICED "BORING" (Walton, and the small-town contrarion)
*(Taught via Sam Walton — richest man in America by doing the boring thing in boring places.)*

**THE SHAPE:** *Chose a market the consensus had literally priced at zero (small-town retail — too small for the chains, too scattered to matter), then made density of exactly that boring market into the unbeatable advantage.*

**THE SITUATION (early 1960s):**
> Discount retail is exploding in big cities, and every smart operator is chasing the hot urban and suburban markets. The "intelligent consensus" is that small towns (pop < 25,000) are dead ends — too thin a customer base, too far from distribution, not worth a store. You are running discount stores in those towns nobody wants. Wal-Mart's later principle is forming.
> **What do you do? Commit before continuing.**

*(Reveal: Section 7, Session 4.)*

**WHO PROVES IT:** Walton (#6, #150, #234) — density of boring small markets; note his radical anti-normal move: he'd fly, drive, walk the stores, look in person, take notes, obsess over costs that others found trivial (#234 describes the approach). Buffett (same family: cheap "boring" insurers). 
**WHO DISPROVES IT:** small-town retail is full of graveyards — the shape only works when density + logistics actually compound; towns that stay dispersed (random towns, no density) kill it.
**YOUR TURN:** *name the market your network/industry treats as "too small to matter." What would change if you treated it as the only market that existed?*



## SESSION 5 — THE BOTTLENECK YOU OWN WITHOUT RUNNING IT (Land, Polaroid; 1940s–60s)
*(Modern founder, but the shape is old. Keep the principle; don't copy the tactic.)*

**THE SHAPE:** *Owned the instant, in-customer's-hands transformation (the film/chemistry) such that the "product" and its consumable were the same locked-in thing — a hardware-plus-consumable monopoly where every unit of hardware self-recruits its consumable.*

**THE SITUATION (1946-ish):**
> You are a physicist turned inventor. Nobody asks for instant photography — film development is a lab chore people expect to wait days for. The big firms own the film market and treat it as a stable commodity on slow cycles. You have a idea for a camera that develops in the user's hands, and a chemistry no one else has. The consensus of the camera industry: people accept the lab; this is a niche curiosity at best, a gimmick real photographers would sneer at.
> **What do you do? Commit before continuing.**

*(Reveal: Section 7, Session 5.)*

**WHO PROVES IT:** Land (#185, and Jobs-interview crossover) — instant the consumable inside the hardware; the same "own the consumable" shape shows up in Gillette-razors, printers+ink, but the *shape* is: the locked-in pair where per-use revenue is huge and the standard is set by the owner. Jobs — same shape, later, in App Store + hardware.
**WHO DISPROVES IT:** the consumable becomes a commodity or the standard is broken by a different medium (Polaroid's instant film died as digital made the *whole* pair redundant — the shape is only permanent if the pair survives the medium).
**FIRES WHEN:** one party can set the interface standard and the consumable is high-margin recurring.
**BACKFIRES WHEN:** a substitute kills the entire pair (not just the consumable) — then the moat is inside a sinking ship.
**YOUR TURN:** *your recurring-revenue line — is it a genuine locked-in pair (hardware+consumable, platform+take-rate) or a service contract that a substitute medium can delete whole?*

# SECTION 7 — THE ANSWER KEY (reading, reveals, and reasoning comparisons)
**Do not read this section while attempting a session. Every session's reveal lives here, away from its prompt.**

## Session 1 — Reveal: Rockefeller
**What he did (the decisive move, literally):** instead of building a cheaper barrel or owning the best crude, he took control of the *link* — railcars, pipelines, and negotiated (and in the period's brutal practice) the transport terms — and made every refiner and producer on both sides of that link dependent on *him*. His margin didn't come from the barrel; it came from the transport term. He then enforced order on a chaotic industry by controlling the one thing everyone needed.
**Your reasoning vs. his:** if you answered "make a cheaper barrel" or "own the oil field," you were right and wrong at once — right that the scattered market was the opportunity, wrong at the *level*: the shape lives one level above the product. Cheaper-barrel is a tactic that dies; the bottleneck is the shape that recurs.
**Evidence note (honest):** corpus contains no Standard-Oil-style *failure* (the brief says this is why counterexample search is always outside-corpus). Outside-corpus candidates to test — railroad/telegraph consolidation failures, such as the numerous failed vertical-integration attempts in railroads themselves; mark CANDIDATE pending actual search, do not treat absence as proof.

## Session 2 — Reveal: Kroc
**What he did:** bought the brothers' operation, then built the *system* as the product — franchising the *rules and the real estate*, not the name. His profit engine was the rent from the land, not the burgers. He owned the bottleneck link (the site/lease) of a scattered category and set terms for everyone in it.
**Your reasoning:** if you said "open more better burger stores" or "buy McDonald's," you got the tactic; if you named *owning the real-estate/rules layer of a scattered category*, you hit the shape.
**The painful version:** being right that "food is local" is exactly the consensus wisdom he ignored at the category level while honoring it at the solution level (local stores, world-wide standards).

## Session 3 — Reveal: Insull
**What happened:** he did NOT stop at owning the link. He pyramided holding companies acquiring downstream utilities with rolling debt, betting on perpetual cheap capital and growth — then 1929 hit: capital stopped rolling, debt matured, equity vaporized, and the entire pyramid collapsed in the biggest utility failure of the era. He defaulted, lost everything, faced fraud charges.

## P1 — THE BORING-LINK OWNER
**SHAPE:** *Took control of the load-bearing link in the chain that everyone on both sides depends on, then set terms for everyone — including people who thought they were his customers and his suppliers. The profit does not come from the product; it comes from the link being owned.*

**LAYER:** permanent (recurring across every century in this corpus)
**EVIDENCE:** Rockefeller — controlled the cost of transportation (pipelines/railcars) in oil, "98 percent of our attention was devoted to the task at hand," prepared for financial emergencies "long before we needed the funds" (#148, #368). Ford — took the supply chain in autos, vertical integration, "aggressively seeking ways to cut production expenses" (#9). Kroc — owned the real-estate/lease + the rules layer of a scattered category, not the burgers (#293). **→ 3 founders, 3 eras (1870s, 1910s, 1950s), 3 industries (oil, autos, food).**
**COUNTEREXAMPLES (outside corpus, search required — mark as candidate pending search):** the failed railroad/telegraph vertical-integration trusts and the 20th-c. conglomerate collapses where owning "the link" became owning the whole chain and diversified into unmanageable sprawl. The Insull counterexample is the strong one and it is *inside* corpus (#336) — see P3.
**FIRES WHEN:** a scattered set of producers+buyers depend on one unglamorous link (transport, credit, distribution, standard) that nobody owns; the link's economics are actually profitable; and you can buy it cheaper than others value it.
**BACKFIRES WHEN:** the link is owned by a monopoly you can't displace; the link is structurally unprofitable; OR you cross the line into financing the whole downstream chain with leverage (→ P3).
**CONFIDENCE:** would change if a corpus founder owned the link and lost for that reason alone (P3 is the closest; it fails at a different point).
**STATUS:** **VALIDATED** (Rockefeller + Ford + Kroc across 3 eras/industries; Insull defines the backfire edge).

## P2 — THE SCATTERED-CATEGORY STANDARDIZER
**SHAPE:** *Standardized a wildly-variable, decentralized category from the top down — owning the rules, the supply, and the distributing link — while the participants fought over the individual unit.*

**LAYER:** permanent
**EVIDENCE:** Kroc — "simplicity and effectiveness of the system," stripped "down to its essence," accomplished "with a minimum of effort"; made the *system* the product (#7, #293). Walton — standardized the scattered small-town retail category and obsessed over the boring machinery: "Our money was made by controlling expenses," "Force himself to know the numbers for every single store" (#234). Ford — standardized the scattered car-build category into the moving line, "a completed Model T rolled off the line every two and a half minutes" (#9). **→ 3 founders, 3 eras, 3 industries.**
**COUNTEREXAMPLES:** the 1960s franchise implosions and every "franchised" brand that licensed a name but never owned the load-bearing asset (rule without asset = no moat). Outside-corpus: failed franchisors.
**FIRES WHEN:** the category is scattered (no standard), the winning unit economics are proven in ONE location, and you can own the reinforcing asset (rules/real-estate/supply).
**BACKFIRES WHEN:** you own the rules but not the asset that pays; OR the category's value is actually *variety* (the standardizer's story is false for that category).
**CONFIDENCE:** would change if a scattered category resisted standardization despite proven unit economics + ownership.
**STATUS:** **VALIDATED.**

**LAYER:** permanent (the backfire edge of P1, not a separate shape — kept as its own veto because it is the single most reliable killer in the corpus)
