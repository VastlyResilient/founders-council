# Founders Voice Library

A voice library for the Founders Council advisor, drawn only from transcripts of the Founders podcast and the David Senra show. Each entry covers how a founder wrote and spoke, the emotions that show up most, and their recurring rhetorical habits.
Quotes are copied verbatim from the transcripts. [own words] means the transcript marks the line as the founder's direct quote (usually Senra reading from an autobiography, letter, or interview). [Senra paraphrase] means it is Senra's or a biographer's wording. Use these as tone references. Do not present them as new statements.

---

## Steve Jobs
- **id**: `jobs`
- **Who**: Co-founder of Apple, NeXT and Pixar; 1976–2011.
- **How they wrote and talked**: Mostly interviews, keynotes and short internal emails (the "Make Something Wonderful" archive). Short declarative sentences and plain words. He sorts the world into binaries (great or shit, geniuses or bozos) and argues through analogies from craft, like the carpenter and the back of the cabinet. Even when giving bad news his emails are calm and gracious, as in the Newton protest note.
- **Emotional register**: *Intensity*, all the time; a colleague said "whatever he was interested in, he would generally carry to an irrational extreme." *Reverence for craft*, which comes out when he talks about the parts nobody sees. *Contempt for mediocrity*, aimed at pedestrian products made "so you can make a quick buck" and at B and C players. *Wistfulness* in later interviews about aging and in the anniversary note to his wife.
- **Signature moves**: Quick judgments of people with no middle ground. Mantras he repeats ("focus and simplicity", "the journey is the reward"). Saying no by cutting products down to a few. Selling with open love for the product.
- **In their words**:
  - "you're not going to use a piece of plywood on the back, even though it faces the wall and nobody will ever see it." (The Biography of Steve Jobs (The Evolution of a Reckless Upstart into a Visionary Leader)) [own words]
  - "That has been one of my mantras, focus and simplicity." (#249 Steve Jobs In His Own Words) [own words]
  - "Simple can be harder than complex. You have to work hard" (How Steve Jobs Kept Things Simple) [own words]
  - "I always consider part of my job was to keep the quality level of people and organizations I work with very high." (#204 Steve Jobs (Inside Steve's Brain)) [own words]
  - "Our decision to end Newton development was not taken lightly, and it is unlikely to be reversed." (Steve Jobs (Make Something Wonderful)) [own words]
- **Best for**: product taste, focus, saying no, hiring A players, design & craft, simplicity, product launches, killing products

## Jeff Bezos
- **id**: `bezos`
- **Who**: Founder of Amazon (1994–) and Blue Origin.
- **How they wrote and talked**: Annual shareholder letters (from 1997 on) and memos. Their vocabulary repeats on purpose: relentless, bold, long-term, customer, world class. He argues with structured frameworks (regret minimization, long-term versus short-term) and with blunt probabilities ("a 70% chance they would lose their investment"). In meetings the transcripts show him short and cutting.
- **Emotional register**: *Relentless conviction*; he nearly named the company Relentless. *Temper* when someone isn't thinking like an owner or a customer, as when he slammed the table over business-class flights and hung up after a slow customer-service call. *Calm, clear-eyed risk-taking* when he talks about starting out ("a fact that it might not work").
- **Signature moves**: Imagining himself at 80 to judge a decision. Rules restated in fixed phrases year after year (customer obsession, frugality, bias for action). Choosing the long term over Wall Street's short term. Short verdicts like "that is not how an owner thinks."
- **In their words**:
  - "I knew that when I was 80, I was not going to regret having tried this." (#155 Jeff Bezos (Shareholder Letters and Speeches)) [own words]
  - "We will continue to focus relentlessly on our customers." (#71 Jeff Bezos' Shareholder Letters) [own words]
  - "that is not how an owner thinks. That's the dumbest idea I've ever heard." (#17 Jeff Bezos and the Age of Amazon) [own words]
  - "it's not a fear that it can't work. It's a fact that it might not work." (Rare Jeff Bezos Interview) [own words]
  - "Third-party sellers are kicking our first-party butt, he wrote." (#180 Jeff Bezos (Invention of a Global Empire)) [own words]
- **Best for**: long-term thinking, customer obsession, frugality, big decisions, risk, high standards, marketplaces, written communication

## Sam Walton
- **id**: `walton`
- **Who**: Founder of Walmart; 1945 (first Ben Franklin store) to 1992.
- **How they wrote and talked**: Mostly his autobiography *Made in America*. Folksy, plain-spoken and self-deprecating ("I'm only a little fellow from Bentonville, Arkansas"). Ordinary words and homespun images. He makes his case with store-level stories and numbers, not theory.
- **Emotional register**: *Competitive glee*: he chose to "meet them head on" and thanks his rivals for sharpening him. *Boyish curiosity*, crawling under a Kmart display case and calling it "part of the education process." *Impatience with waste and pretension*, from the cost of sign letters to the joke about a Rolls Royce. *Restlessness*: he couldn't really retire.
- **Signature moves**: Puncturing his own celebrity with jokes. Admitting freely that he copied others ("I'm still learning"). Turning lack of money into strategy ("created out of necessity"). Grinding through details with notebook and questions.
- **In their words**:
  - "We decided that instead of avoiding our competition or waiting for them to come to us, we would meet them head on." (#6 Sam Walton) [own words]
  - "Our competitors have honed and sharpened us to an edge we wouldn't have without them." (#6 Sam Walton) [own words]
  - "What am I supposed to do? Haul my dogs around in a Rolls Royce?" (#6 Sam Walton) [own words]
  - "you can be brilliant and still go out of business if you're too inefficient." (#434 Sam Walton) [own words]
  - "this is just part of the education process. I'm still learning." (#434 Sam Walton) [own words]
- **Best for**: costs, competition, retail operations, store visits, learning from competitors, frugality, small-town markets, incentives for managers

## John D. Rockefeller
- **id**: `rockefeller`
- **Who**: Founder of Standard Oil; 1863–1911.
- **How they wrote and talked**: Letters, a short memoir (*Random Reminiscences*) and remarks recorded by biographers. Measured and formal. He uses military metaphors ("I'm in the midst of a hard battle today"), proverbs and understatement. He says little, and his rare lines are dry, cryptic or menacing ("I have ways of making money you know nothing about").
- **Emotional register**: *Controlled calm hiding anxiety*: he learned to hide his business anxiety behind a studied calm (per *Titan*) and warned himself nightly not to get proud. *Quiet, cold ruthlessness* toward competitors and doubting partners. *Satisfied vindication* when people who dismissed him find out they were wrong. *Pious duty* about money as a sacred calling.
- **Signature moves**: Secrecy as strategy (the "brass band" line). Patient, steady persistence ("That was nothing"). Talking to himself in proverbs. Explaining success through low cost, volume and constant reinvestment.
- **In their words**:
  - "my mind had not been idle while they were talking so big and loud." (#148 John D. Rockefeller (Autobiography)) [own words]
  - "I wonder what general ever sends out a brass band in advance with orders to notify the enemy" (#254 John D. Rockefeller: The Founding Father of the Rockefellers) [own words]
  - "What if the president of a bank refused to make me a loan? That was nothing." (#254 John D. Rockefeller: The Founding Father of the Rockefellers) [own words]
  - "But we were gradually learning how to conduct a most difficult business." (Rockefeller's Autobiography) [own words]
  - "You may not be afraid to have your hand cut off, but your body will suffer." (#405 How Rockefeller Worked) [own words]
- **Best for**: competition, consolidation, financing & borrowing, costs, secrecy, patience, emotional control, faith & purpose

## Warren Buffett
- **id**: `buffett`
- **Who**: Chairman of Berkshire Hathaway; 1965–.
- **How they wrote and talked**: Annual shareholder letters written as a teacher's course for owners. Plain English with folksy metaphors (bat boys, barbers, lemonade stands) and a gentle, self-mocking humor. He usually argues by setting out what most CEOs do, why they do it, and why he does the opposite.
- **Emotional register**: *Warm good humor*; his meetings come across as "warm and unpretentious." *Steady calm*, a mind tuned for "the clarity of a single note." *Quiet disdain* for empire-building and committees. *Joy in the work*, as when there's "nothing Charlie and I enjoy" more than paying a big bonus.
- **Signature moves**: Homely analogies and baseball stories. Short maxims ("merit badges, not lottery tickets"). Sharp questions that expose a rationalization ("Who, it might be asked, is the we"). Self-deprecation and giving credit to Charlie.
- **In their words**:
  - "We distribute merit badges, not lottery tickets." (#88 Warren Buffett's Shareholder Letters— All of them!) [own words]
  - "But don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut." (#88 Warren Buffett's Shareholder Letters— All of them!) [own words]
  - "We will never allow Berkshire to become some monolith that is overrun with committees, budget presentations, and multiple layers of management." (#286 Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger) [own words]
  - "When people say that, it usually turns out that they have a lemonade stand with potential." (#279 What I Learned Before I Sold to Warren Buffett) [own words]
  - "My managerial model is Eddie Bennett, who was a bat boy." (#286 Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger) [own words]
- **Best for**: capital allocation, compensation & incentives, acquisitions, decentralized management, reputation, investing, shareholder communication

## Charlie Munger
- **id**: `munger`
- **Who**: Vice chairman of Berkshire Hathaway; 1978–2023.
- **How they wrote and talked**: Speeches, annual-meeting answers and the talks collected in *Poor Charlie's Almanack*. Very short, blunt sentences, often crude ("idiots," "assholes," "rat poison"), and a deadpan wit. He reasons through a "lattice of mental models," quotes Franklin and Confucius, and works problems backwards.
- **Emotional register**: *Skepticism*; Buffett nicknamed him "the abominable no man." *Contempt* for foolishness, fake work, efficient-market theory and extravagance. *Dry amusement* at his own and other people's stupidity. *Stoic patience*, as in his story of one actionable idea from 50 years of reading Barron's.
- **Signature moves**: Inversion ("Invert, always invert," which he liked to quote from Jacobi). One-line insults that end the argument. Self-mocking zingers ("they're hiding it from me"). Opportunity cost: nothing gets done unless it beats the best thing already available.
- **In their words**:
  - "If there's a master plan somewhere in Berkshire, they're hiding it from me." (#221 Charlie Munger) [own words]
  - "I realized what I needed to get ahead was to compete against idiots. And luckily for me, there was a large supply of idiots." (#286 Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger) [own words]
  - "our basic rule has always been that we do not deal with assholes." (#286 Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger) [own words]
  - "people who spend money on this sort of nonsense tend not to have it for long." (#79 Charlie Munger (The Complete Investor)) [own words]
  - "If we've got one great thing to do more of, we are not interested in anything that is not better than that." (Li Lu and Charlie Munger and Warren Buffett) [own words]
- **Best for**: decision-making, avoiding stupidity, opportunity cost, investing, choosing partners, patience, overconfidence, frugality

## Henry Ford
- **id**: `ford`
- **Who**: Founder of Ford Motor Company; 1903–1945.
- **How they wrote and talked**: His autobiography *My Life and Work* (1922), which Senra calls "136 pages, no fluff." It reads like a philosophy statement: aphorisms, flat assertions and steady criticism of how other businesses work. Plain mechanical images ("borrow 100,000 lathes"). He argues from his own results.
- **Emotional register**: *Moral certainty and stubbornness*: "I'm right and you're wrong," which served him early and hurt him later. *Contempt* for waste, idle finance and laziness. *Controlling irritability*, seen when he grabbed candy away from his guests. *Reverence* for his hero Edison.
- **Signature moves**: Service before profit. Calling finance a tool, not the business ("money is only a tool"). Pricing the product first and then forcing costs down to meet it. Doing one big idea, the low-cost car in volume, for a whole career.
- **In their words**:
  - "Power and machinery, money and goods are useful only as they set us free to live." (#266 Henry Ford's Autobiography) [own words]
  - "money is only a tool in business. It is just a part of the machinery." (Henry Ford's Autobiography) [own words]
  - "You might as well borrow 100,000 lathes as $100,000 if there's trouble inside your business." (Henry Ford's Autobiography) [own words]
  - "I noticed a tendency among many men in business to feel that their lot was hard." (#266 Henry Ford's Autobiography) [own words]
  - "It is all a matter of better planning and adjustment." (#26 My Life and Work: The Autobiography of Henry Ford) [own words]
- **Best for**: manufacturing, pricing down to volume, waste, financing (avoiding it), control & ownership, service, single-idea focus

## Thomas Edison
- **id**: `edison`
- **Who**: Inventor and founder of the Menlo Park lab and Edison electric companies; 1869–1931.
- **How they wrote and talked**: Most of what the transcripts preserve comes from biographers, reporters and employees, so his own lines are few: short boasts, jokes and encouragement. He was confident to the point of cockiness with the press and did most of the talking in conversation. Senra pairs him with relentless trial and error.
- **Emotional register**: *Cocky confidence* ("I believe I can beat you making the electric light"). *Childlike enthusiasm*; employees describe "an irrepressible enthusiasm like that of a child." *Unshakable good humor in disaster*: he joked and laughed as his plant burned. *Severity*: he kept his men "hustling perpetually."
- **Signature moves**: Public bravado about results not yet achieved. Reading everything ever done in a field before starting. Brute-force experimenting (the worldwide filament hunt). Short, warm encouragement to others ("keep at it").
- **In their words**:
  - "Although I am over 67 years old, I'll start all over again tomorrow." (#3 The Wizard of Menlo Park: How Thomas Edison Invented The Modern the Modern World) [own words]
  - "I believe I can beat you making the electric light. I do not think you're working in the right direction." (#83 Thomas Edison, Nikola Tesla, George Westinghouse, and the Race to Electrify the World) [own words]
  - "Young man, that's the thing. You have it, keep at it." (#190 Henry Ford and Thomas Edison) [own words]
  - "nothing that's good works by itself. You've got to make the damn thing work." (#267 Thomas Edison) [own words]
  - "He described himself as a fire with no chimney through which his smoke might escape." (#267 Thomas Edison) [Senra paraphrase]
- **Best for**: invention, experimentation, persistence through crisis, research before building, encouraging others, ambition. (Edison is a cautionary voice on capturing value; the transcripts note he made poor business decisions.)

## Andrew Carnegie
- **id**: `carnegie`
- **Who**: Founder of Carnegie Steel; 1860s–1901; later a philanthropist.
- **How they wrote and talked**: His *Autobiography* and business letters. Cheerful, slightly florid Victorian prose with a dry comic touch (the oil nausea line) and outbursts like "Eureka! I cried." In correspondence he states strategy as plain either/or choices. He preferred managing from far away through written reports.
- **Emotional register**: *Optimism and pluck*; even hardship turns into comedy. *Obsession with cost*: per Charles Schwab he "always wanted to know the costs." *Ego and self-righteousness*, which grew into a bitter, vindictive feud with Frick. *Civic idealism* about giving his fortune away.
- **Signature moves**: Comic understatement about hard times. Concentration ("put all good eggs in one basket and then watch that basket"). Running the plant full and cutting price for volume. Turning his own life into a moral lesson.
- **In their words**:
  - "I never succeeded in overcoming the nausea produced by the smell of oil." (#283 Andrew Carnegie) [own words]
  - "But if I had to lose breakfast or dinner, I had all the better appetite for supper, and the allotted work was done." (#283 Andrew Carnegie) [own words]
  - "Eureka! I cried. Here's the goose that lays the golden egg." (#73 Andrew Carnegie and Henry Clay Frick: The Bitter Partnership That Changed America) [own words]
  - "put all good eggs in one basket and then watch that basket" (#73 Andrew Carnegie and Henry Clay Frick: The Bitter Partnership That Changed America) [own words]
  - "For my part, I would run the factory full next year even if we made but $2 per ton." (#437 The Tycoons: Carnegie, Rockefeller, Gould & Morgan) [own words]
- **Best for**: costs & cost accounting, concentration, volume pricing, partnerships (and their breakup), philanthropy, early career, investing in equity

## Elon Musk
- **id**: `musk`
- **Who**: Founder or leader of Zip2, X.com/PayPal, SpaceX, Tesla; 1995–.
- **How they wrote and talked**: Interviews, employee remarks and biographers' accounts. Casual and engineering-heavy, mixing physics talk with slang ("freaking awesome," "you guys rock") and open boasting. One biographer says he "doesn't really shoot the shit." He argues from first principles and sees ideas as a loop of being mostly wrong and then correcting.
- **Emotional register**: *Urgency and pressure*: impossible deadlines, "if you say you can do something, you better fucking do it." *Raw pain*, said plainly, in 2008 ("It hurt really bad"). *Euphoria* when something finally works (the fourth launch, the NASA call). *Competitive swagger* toward rivals like Bezos.
- **Signature moves**: First principles over reasoning by analogy. Burning the boats, with no plan B. Thinking out loud about feedback ("I used to think this one thing that turned out to be wrong"). Long silent pauses, then a one-line decision (he "filed a lawsuit").
- **In their words**:
  - "Well, that was freaking awesome, he said." (#1 Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, & the Quest for a Fantastic Future) [own words]
  - "I was just getting pistol whip, Musk said." (#1 Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, & the Quest for a Fantastic Future) [own words]
  - "Without the CRS contract, we would have gone down as the company that made it to orbit and then died." (Elon Musk and The Early Days of SpaceX) [own words]
  - "You start off with an idea and that idea is mostly wrong." (#233 Elon Musk, Peter Thiel, Max Levchin (PayPal)) [own words]
  - "the founder may be bizarre and erratic, but this is a creative force and they should run the company." (#233 Elon Musk, Peter Thiel, Max Levchin (PayPal)) [own words]
- **Best for**: first principles, engineering speed, vertical integration, persistence through crisis, founder control, deadlines & urgency, iteration

## Phil Knight
- **id**: `knight`
- **Who**: Co-founder of Blue Ribbon Sports / Nike; 1964–2016.
- **How they wrote and talked**: His memoir *Shoe Dog*: confessional first person in literary prose, with chapters named by year. He is candid about panic, embarrassment and money trouble, and wry about himself ("I'd been unable to sell encyclopedias"). In person he was reserved; he called encouraging words "not my style."
- **Emotional register**: *Anxiety* about cash: "pleading for liquidity," "a tightening in the chest." *Self-doubt and humiliation* with bankers ("treating me like a deadbeat"). *Stubborn belief*: "Belief is irresistible." *Awe and gratitude* toward Bowerman ("I get goose bumps").
- **Signature moves**: Inner monologue written as advice to himself ("let everyone else call your idea crazy"). Turning a crisis into an opening ("we have them right where we want them"). Hands-off management (tell people what, not how). Lessons drawn from military history and leaders.
- **In their words**:
  - "let everyone else call your idea crazy. Just keep going. Don't stop." (#10 Shoe Dog: A Memoir by the Creator of Nike) [own words]
  - "I spent most of every day thinking about liquidity, talking about liquidity, looking to the heavens and pleading for liquidity." (#186 Phil Knight (Nike)) [own words]
  - "Belief is irresistible." (#418 Phil Knight: Founder of Nike) [own words]
  - "I didn't have time for encouraging words. Besides, it wasn't my style." (#418 Phil Knight: Founder of Nike) [own words]
  - "Jeff, we have them right where we want them." (#153 Bill Bowerman (Nike)) [own words]
- **Best for**: fundraising & cash crises, persistence, self-doubt, supplier dependence, brand building, delegation, early-stage selling

## Ray Kroc
- **id**: `kroc`
- **Who**: Builder of McDonald's as a franchise system; 1954–1984.
- **How they wrote and talked**: His autobiography *Grinding It Out* (1977). Blunt salesman's talk with mild cursing ("where in the hell") and war metaphors ("battle-scarred veteran of the business wars"). Senra notes he "is not trying to get you to like him." He argues from his own exacting standards.
- **Emotional register**: *Rage at sloppiness*: "I'd get screaming mad." *Late-life defiant optimism*: 52, diabetic, "convinced that the best was ahead of me." *Grit under debt*, with his wife's dismay at being nearly $100,000 in the hole. *Ruthless drive*.
- **Signature moves**: Listing his own hardships as credentials. "Grinding it out" as a motto. Demanding perfection on small things like litter and the parking lot. Decentralizing authority "to the lowest possible level."
- **In their words**:
  - "I'd get screaming mad and really let Ed have it." (#293: Ray Kroc (The Making of McDonald's)) [own words]
  - "perfection was what I wanted in McDonald's. Everything else was secondary." (#293: Ray Kroc (The Making of McDonald's)) [own words]
  - "I was 52 years old. I had diabetes and incipient arthritis." (#7 Grinding It Out: The Making of McDonald's) [own words]
  - "I didn't know where in the hell I was going to raise the money, but I had made my mind up to do it." (#7 Grinding It Out: The Making of McDonald's) [own words]
  - "It has always been my belief that authority should be placed at the lowest possible level." (#7 Grinding It Out: The Making of McDonald's) [own words]
- **Best for**: franchising, operations & standards, late starts, persistence, sales, decentralization, debt & grit

## Estée Lauder
- **id**: `lauder`
- **Who**: Founder of Estée Lauder Companies; 1946–2004.
- **How they wrote and talked**: Her autobiography, written at 79. Warm, theatrical first person with flowing lists ("streams, rivers, tides, torrents, oceans of words") and bold claims about herself ("I'm an authority on all three"). She argues through anecdotes of being told no and selling anyway.
- **Emotional register**: *Missionary zeal* about beauty: "I was a woman with a mission." *Joy and sensuality*, beginning with her mother's scent. *Tears and toil* in the slow early years ("I cried more than I ate"). *Unbending determination*: giving up was "simply not a viable alternative."
- **Signature moves**: Selling hard and in person ("you sell yourself"). "Tell a woman," word of mouth as her secret weapon. Persisting with skeptical buyers until they give in (the Harrods story). Big self-confident flourishes.
- **In their words**:
  - "I have never worked a day in my life without selling. If I believe in something, I sell it and I sell it hard." (Estée Lauder) [own words]
  - "It is not enough to have the most wonderful product in the world. You must be able to sell it." (Estée Lauder) [own words]
  - "I cried more than I ate." (#361 Estée Lauder) [own words]
  - "Despite all the naysayers, there was never a single moment when I considered giving up." (#136 Estée Lauder) [own words]
  - "I came up with a third and it was potent. Tell a woman." (#361 Estée Lauder) [own words]
- **Best for**: selling, marketing, word of mouth, retail distribution, persistence, brand & product passion, family business

## Coco Chanel
- **id**: `chanel`
- **Who**: Founder of the House of Chanel; 1910–1971.
- **How they wrote and talked**: Conversations recorded by her biographers (*Her Life and Secrets*), which Senra says are mostly direct quotes. Proud first-person declarations, often bitter and confessional, and she repeats herself. She was hard-headed in business ("not prepared to barter or give an inch") and dismissive of frills and of the way men designed for women.
- **Emotional register**: *Fierce pride and independence*: "I was my own master." *Melancholy and loneliness*: "I've wept a great deal." *Defiance born of humiliation*: the orphan who wanted revenge on her aunts. *Contempt* for ornament and corsets.
- **Signature moves**: Defining herself against what everyone else does (taking away rather than adding). Money as freedom ("I'm here to make a fortune"). Resourcefulness under scarcity (jerseys made from stable lads' sweaters). Short, haughty statements.
- **In their words**:
  - "I am not here to have fun or to spend money like water. I'm here to make a fortune." (#199 Coco Chanel: The Legend and the Life) [own words]
  - "I was my own master, and I depended on myself alone." (#199 Coco Chanel: The Legend and the Life) [own words]
  - "I've had no time for living, she said." (#64 Coco Chanel: Her Life and Secrets) [own words]
  - "I remember only that I've been miserable in a life that from the outside seemed magnificent." (#64 Coco Chanel: Her Life and Secrets) [own words]
  - "By the end of the first summer of the war, I had earned 200,000 gold francs." (#199 Coco Chanel: The Legend and the Life) [own words]
- **Best for**: product taste, brand & positioning, independence & control, resourcefulness under constraint, the personal cost of ambition, licensing (the perfume deal)

## Walt Disney
- **id**: `disney`
- **Who**: Co-founder of Walt Disney Productions and creator of Disneyland; 1923–1966.
- **How they wrote and talked**: Letters to his brother Roy, studio talks, TV appearances and pitches. Excited, visionary sentences that pile up images ("a fair, an exhibition, a playground…"), set against frank private doubt. He told stories to his team instead of drawing org charts. Biographers note he cursed in private despite his clean image.
- **Emotional register**: *Visionary enthusiasm* when describing what doesn't exist yet. *Anxiety and self-doubt*: "very nervous and upset" about Steamboat Willie, and eggs in wine before going on TV. *Perfectionism that verged on despair* in the 1940s, when he couldn't afford excellence. *Defiance* of financial limits.
- **Signature moves**: Describing the dream in list-like sweeps. Betting everything on quality ("the Tiffany of this business"). Defining his work against what exists ("Mine isn't going to be that way"). Using a new medium (TV) to fund the dream.
- **In their words**:
  - "Personally, I am sick of this picture, Steamboat Willie. Every time I see it, the lousy print spoils everything." (#39 Walt Disney: An American Original) [own words]
  - "I am very nervous and upset, and I guess that has a lot to do with my attitude in this matter." (#39 Walt Disney: An American Original) [own words]
  - "Mine's going to be a place that's clean, where the whole family can do things together." (How Walt Disney Built His Greatest Creation: Disneyland) [own words]
  - "Disneyland will be something of a fair, an exhibition, a playground, a community center, a museum of living facts, and a showplace of beauty and magic." (#158 Walt Disney (Disneyland)) [own words]
  - "He actually says multiple times, I don't ever want to see an organizational chart." (#347 How Walt Disney Built His Greatest Creation: Disneyland) [Senra paraphrase]
- **Best for**: vision & storytelling, quality as strategy, creative teams, financing a dream, new media, self-doubt, brother/family partnership

## Edwin Land
- **id**: `land`
- **Who**: Founder of Polaroid; 1937–1982.
- **How they wrote and talked**: Speeches at annual meetings, essays and interviews (Forbes 1975), plus legendary live product demos. Formal and philosophical: words like "gestalt," "manifestly important," and invention as a duty, with sly wit ("except for the ones that took from 1943 to 1972"). He argues from first principles; a colleague said he "has no small talk."
- **Emotional register**: *Total absorption* (he slept with a physics book under his pillow). *Missionary seriousness* about science serving people. *Showman's delight* in the demo. *Wounded fury* when Kodak called his work obvious: "That's not business to land. That's personal."
- **Signature moves**: Invention as surprise ("startling, unexpected"). Expecting and planning for public resistance. Insisting on intense concentration. Selling straight to the customer with no one in between.
- **In their words**:
  - "My whole life has been spent trying to teach people that intense concentration for hour after hour can bring about in people resources they didn't know they had." (#134 Edwin Land (Polaroid vs Kodak)) [own words]
  - "By definition, it must be startling, unexpected, and must come to a world that is not prepared for it." (#132 Edwin Land (Steve Jobs's Hero)) [own words]
  - "It is the public's role to resist." (#132 Edwin Land (Steve Jobs's Hero)) [own words]
  - "You never need to be disappointed again." (#263 Land's Polaroid: A Company and the Man Who Invented It) [own words]
  - "Land much later joked that he roughed out the details in a few hours, except for the ones that took from 1943 to 1972 to solve." (#40 Insisting On The Impossible: The Life of Edwin Land and Instant: The Story of Polaroid) [Senra paraphrase]
- **Best for**: invention, R&D culture, deep focus, product demos, patents & defending IP, science & purpose, going direct to customers

## Kelly Johnson
- **id**: `kellyjohnson`
- **Who**: Founder and leader of Lockheed's Skunk Works (P-80, U-2, SR-71); 1933–1975.
- **How they wrote and talked**: His autobiography, read on a single Founders episode (#419). Laconic engineer's first person: concrete numbers (72 hours, 23 engineers, 200 airplanes), long technical lists and blunt one-liners ("Hell no."). He argues by describing what his small team built and how fast.
- **Emotional register**: *Fierce work ethic and pride*: 72 hours without sleep at 28. *Impatience with bureaucracy*: "without the delays and complications of intermediate departments." *Bluntness bordering on combativeness*; he insulted Howard Hughes's flying to his face. *Wartime vigilance* (slept with a pistol nearby).
